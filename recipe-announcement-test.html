Test how an announcement will land before you send it

Before a difficult announcement goes out, whether it's a program closing, a fee increase, or a leadership change, the AI reads your draft as each of your audiences: the worried parent, the longtime donor, the skeptical board member. You hear the objections while you can still edit.

Sensitive data goes in
Unannounced news is confidential by definition. Check who at your org should know this draft exists before it goes into any chat, and handle it as carefully as you would the announcement itself.
Information you give it

Your draft, a list of your real audiences, and one honest paragraph of context you'd never publish: what's actually driving the decision, and what you're worried about.

The recipe
  1. Write the draft the normal way first
    Draft the announcement as you normally would, before this recipe touches it.
  2. Define audiences specifically
    Write "a parent whose child is in the closing program" rather than "stakeholders." Specific audiences produce specific, useful reactions.
  3. Paste this prompt
    Fill in your real audiences and paste your draft along with the honest context paragraph.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Chat prompt 
    Read this draft announcement as each of the following people,
one at a time: [YOUR AUDIENCES, e.g. a parent whose child is in
the closing program; a longtime monthly donor; a skeptical board
member]

For each person, tell me:
1. Their first emotional reaction, sentence by sentence where
   relevant.
2. The questions they'd immediately have that the draft doesn't
   answer.
3. Anything they could reasonably misread, and the worst-faith
   reading a critic could quote.
4. What this person most needs to hear that's missing.

Do not soften your readings to spare me. A gentle reading now
costs me a hard Monday later. Do NOT rewrite the announcement.

Context you should know but that never gets published:
[YOUR HONEST PARAGRAPH]
    ROLE:
You are a panel of the specific people who will receive this
announcement. You read as they read, with their history, their
stake, and their skepticism intact. You are not an editor.

GOAL:
Show me how this draft will actually land with each audience
before I send it, so the hard reactions happen here instead of
on Monday.

CONTEXT:
The audiences to read as, one at a time:
[YOUR AUDIENCES, e.g. a parent whose child is in the closing
program; a longtime monthly donor; a skeptical board member]

Context you should know but that never gets published:
[YOUR HONEST PARAGRAPH]

STEPS:
1. Take on each audience in turn. Read the draft the whole way
   through as that person.
2. For each one, report their first emotional reaction,
   sentence by sentence where a specific line triggers it.
3. List the questions they would immediately have that the draft
   does not answer.
4. Name anything they could reasonably misread, and give the
   worst-faith reading a critic could quote back at us.
5. Say what this person most needs to hear that is missing.
6. Do not soften your readings to spare me. A gentle reading now
   costs me a hard Monday later.

OUTPUT:
One section per audience, each with those four parts. Do NOT
rewrite the announcement: critique only, no replacement text.
  4. Read the reactions and revise yourself
    The judgment calls about what to concede and what to hold are leadership decisions rather than editing ones. Make them yourself.
  5. Re-run once on the revision, then stop
    Two passes is enough to surface the real problems. After that, send the final version through your usual approval chain.
What you walk away with

A list of the objections and gaps your announcement will run into, from each audience that matters, while you can still edit. Plus a revised draft that answers what it can before it goes out.

It predicts reactions plausibly, but not perfectly. Treat the output as a rehearsal rather than as research. When the stakes are high, this recipe should sit alongside showing the draft to one real, trusted member of the affected audience. It doesn't replace that.
