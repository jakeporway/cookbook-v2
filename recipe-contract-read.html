Review a contract before you sign it

The venue contract, the office lease, the insurance renewal, read closely and returned in plain language: what you're committing to, what's unusual for this kind of agreement, and the questions to ask before signing. This prepares you for the decision. It isn't legal advice.

Sensitive data goes in
A draft contract is a business document, so this is usually fine. Check the draft for a confidentiality clause before pasting it anywhere, including into a chat.
Information you give it

The contract PDF, plus one sentence of context, such as "we're a 12-person nonprofit renting this space for a one-day event."

The recipe
  1. Attach the contract and add context
    Start a new chat, attach the contract PDF, and add one sentence about who you are and what the agreement is for.
  2. Paste this prompt
    Swap in the type of agreement it is.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Chat prompt 
    Read this contract completely and give me:
1. Every obligation we take on, in plain language, with the
   section cited for each.
2. Every obligation the other party takes on, cited.
3. Dates, deadlines, auto-renewals, and notice periods, cited.
4. Anything unusual, one-sided, or missing compared to a
   standard [TYPE OF AGREEMENT], and why it's worth attention.
5. The five most useful questions to ask before signing.

If a section is ambiguous, say it's ambiguous. Do not resolve
ambiguity in either side's favor. You are helping me prepare, not
giving legal advice.
    ROLE:
You are a careful reader preparing me to review a contract. You
are not my lawyer and you do not give legal advice: you make
sure I know what is in the document before I talk to someone who
does.

GOAL:
A complete, cited map of what this contract commits each side to,
and the questions worth asking before I sign.

CONTEXT:
The contract is attached. Treat it as a [TYPE OF AGREEMENT] and
compare it against what is standard for that kind of agreement.

STEPS:
1. Read the contract completely before summarizing anything.
2. List every obligation we take on, in plain language, with the
   section cited for each.
3. List every obligation the other party takes on, cited.
4. Pull out all dates, deadlines, auto-renewals, and notice
   periods, cited.
5. Flag anything unusual, one-sided, or missing compared to a
   standard [TYPE OF AGREEMENT], and say why it is worth
   attention.
6. Where a section is ambiguous, say it is ambiguous. Do not
   resolve ambiguity in either side's favor.

OUTPUT:
Those five sections in order, every point carrying its section
citation, ending with the five most useful questions to ask
before signing.
  3. Read the flagged sections yourself in the original
    The cited sections make this fast. Go straight to what was flagged rather than rereading the whole contract from the top.
  4. Ask the other party your questions
    Most unusual clauses have an ordinary explanation, and asking costs nothing. Bring the five questions to the conversation.
  5. Know the escalation line
    If the contract involves real money, real liability, or anything you'd lose sleep over, a lawyer should review it. Many state nonprofit associations and legal-aid clinics offer low-cost contract review. This recipe makes that hour more efficient; it doesn't make it unnecessary.
What you walk away with

A plain-language map of the contract: obligations on both sides, deadlines, and the unusual clauses, with a citation behind every claim, and five questions ready for the other party.

This is a reading aid, not legal counsel. It makes you well prepared for the negotiation, but it doesn't replace someone who has been to law school. Knowing when to escalate is part of the recipe.
