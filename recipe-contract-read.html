Review a contract before you sign it

The venue contract, the office lease, the insurance renewal — read closely and returned in plain language: what you're committing to, what's unusual for this kind of agreement, and the questions to ask before signing. Preparation for a decision, not legal advice.

Information you give it

The contract PDF, plus one sentence of context — "we're a 12-person nonprofit renting this space for a one-day event."

The recipe
  1. Attach the contract and add context
    Start a new chat, attach the contract PDF, and add one sentence about who you are and what the agreement is for.
  2. Paste this prompt
    Swap in the type of agreement it is.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Chat prompt
    Read this contract completely and give me:
1. Every obligation we take on, in plain language, with the
   section cited for each.
2. Every obligation the other party takes on, cited.
3. Dates, deadlines, auto-renewals, and notice periods, cited.
4. Anything unusual, one-sided, or missing compared to a
   standard [TYPE OF AGREEMENT], and why it's worth attention.
5. The five most useful questions to ask before signing.

If a section is ambiguous, say it's ambiguous — do not resolve
ambiguity in either side's favor. You are helping me prepare, not
giving legal advice.
  3. Read the flagged sections yourself in the original
    The cited sections make this fast — go straight to what was flagged rather than rereading the whole contract cold.
  4. Ask the other party your questions
    Most "unusual" clauses have a boring explanation, and asking is free. Bring the five questions to the conversation.
  5. Know the escalation line
    Real money, real liability, or anything you'd lose sleep over → a lawyer reviews it. Many state nonprofit associations and legal-aid clinics offer low-cost contract review — this recipe makes that hour efficient, not unnecessary.
What you walk away with

A plain-language map of the contract — obligations on both sides, deadlines, and the unusual clauses — with a citation behind every claim, and five questions ready for the other party.

This is a reading aid, not counsel. It makes you the best-prepared person in the negotiation who hasn't been to law school — it does not replace the person who has. The escalation line is part of the recipe, not an afterthought.

What's making you pause on this one?
The venue says their contract is "standard, everyone signs it."
Run it anyway. "Standard" contracts contain the same auto-renewal and indemnification surprises as the rest. If it truly is standard, the review takes ten minutes and costs nothing.
