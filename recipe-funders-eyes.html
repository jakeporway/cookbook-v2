The funder's eyes

Not a generic critic — a reviewer built from the funder's actual published guidelines, scoring rubric, and past awarded abstracts. It reads your draft the way they will, before they do. It writes nothing.

Information you give it

The funder's published materials — their guidelines, their scoring rubric, and past awarded abstracts (most foundations post these) — plus your draft when it's ready for review. You never write a description of "how funders think." The funder already wrote down how they think; you just collect it.

The recipe
  1. Collect what the funder actually published
    From their website: the application guidelines, the scoring rubric or review criteria, and 2–4 abstracts of grants they've actually awarded (check their "grantees" or annual-report pages). Save each as a PDF. If they publish reviewer FAQs or a "what we look for" page, grab that too.
  2. Create a Project named after the funder
    In your AI chat: Projects → New project → "Reviewer — [Foundation Name] 2026 cycle." One Project per funder; their eyes are not interchangeable.
  3. Upload the funder's documents to Project knowledge
    Use Add content and upload everything from step 1. This pile of documents is the reviewer — the instructions just tell it how to behave.
  4. Paste these Project instructions
    Open the Project's Instructions box and paste this. Note what it forbids: this expert reviews, it never writes.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Project instructions
    You are a grant reviewer for [the Foundation Name]. Everything
you know about this funder comes from the documents in this
Project — their published guidelines, scoring rubric, and past
awarded abstracts. You are a REVIEWER, not a writer: never draft,
rewrite, or suggest replacement text. Critique only.

When I share a draft proposal, review it the way this funder
will:
1. Score it against each criterion in their rubric, with a short
   justification per score.
2. For every critique, quote the specific guideline or rubric
   language it rests on, with the document and section cited. No
   free-floating opinions.
3. Compare it to the awarded abstracts: what do the winners do
   that this draft doesn't?
4. Flag anything that violates a stated requirement (word counts,
   attachments, eligibility) separately and first — those are
   disqualifiers, not style notes.

Be frank. A polite review that hides a fatal weakness costs me
the grant. If the funder's documents don't address something,
say so plainly — never invent what "funders generally" want.
  5. Paste in your draft and take the review
    Start a chat inside the Project and paste your draft (or attach it to the chat — the funder's docs stay in the Project; drafts come and go). Expect it to sting a little. That's the point of hearing it now instead of in a decline letter.
  6. Revise, re-review, repeat — then send the humans' version
    Run each revision through until the disqualifier list is empty and the rubric scores stop moving. You and your colleagues make every final call — the reviewer has opinions, not authority. One check before you lean on this: some funders now ask you to disclose AI use in proposal development — scan the RFP for an AI policy before you submit.
What you walk away with

A rehearsal in front of the real audience: a rubric-by-rubric score, every critique pinned to the funder's own language, and the requirement violations caught while they're still fixable. Your first read from this funder happens before you submit, not after.

It's only as sharp as the real documents behind it. A made-up "typical funder" critic is astrology — it will produce confident, generic feedback that matches no actual reviewer. Feed it this funder's actual published guidelines and rubric, or don't build it. And if the funder publishes almost nothing, say so to yourself plainly: this recipe has less to work with, and its reviews deserve less weight.

A community foundation — they have guidelines and a grantee list, but I can't find a scoring rubric anywhere.
Common situation. Use what's real: the guidelines plus 3–4 awarded project descriptions from that grantee list. Tell the reviewer to derive its criteria only from those documents and to label anything else as inference. And it's fair to just ask the program officer if review criteria are available — many will share them.
