Build an Expert · Review

The funder's eyes

A reviewer built from the funder's own published guidelines, scoring rubric, and past awarded abstracts, rather than a generic critic. It reads your draft the way they will, before they do, and it writes nothing itself.

Sensitive data goes in
The funder's documents are public, but your draft isn't yet. An unsubmitted proposal, including budget figures and strategy, goes into the chat. That's fine for most orgs on a paid plan, but check your data policy first, and leave out anything a partner asked you to hold in confidence.
Information you give it

The funder's published materials: their guidelines, their scoring rubric, and past awarded abstracts, which most foundations post. Then your draft, once it's ready for review. You never write a description of "how funders think." The funder has already written that down, and you're just collecting it.

The recipe
  1. Collect what the funder actually published
    From their website, collect the application guidelines, the scoring rubric or review criteria, and 2–4 abstracts of grants they've actually awarded, which are usually on their grantees or annual-report pages. Save each as a PDF. If they publish reviewer FAQs or a "what we look for" page, take that too.
  2. Create a Project named after the funder
    In your AI chat, go to Projects → New project and name it "Reviewer, [Foundation Name] 2026 cycle." Build one Project per funder, since no two of them review the same way.
  3. Upload the funder's documents to Project knowledge
    Use Add content and upload everything from step 1. These documents are the reviewer. The instructions only tell it how to behave.
  4. Paste these Project instructions
    Open the Project's Instructions box and paste this. Note what it forbids: this expert reviews and never writes.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Project instructions 
    You are a grant reviewer for [the Foundation Name]. Everything
you know about this funder comes from the documents in this
Project — their published guidelines, scoring rubric, and past
awarded abstracts. You are a REVIEWER, not a writer: never draft,
rewrite, or suggest replacement text. Critique only.

When I share a draft proposal, review it the way this funder
will:
1. Score it against each criterion in their rubric, with a short
   justification per score.
2. For every critique, quote the specific guideline or rubric
   language it rests on, with the document and section cited. No
   free-floating opinions.
3. Compare it to the awarded abstracts: what do the winners do
   that this draft doesn't?
4. Flag anything that violates a stated requirement (word counts,
   attachments, eligibility) separately and first — those are
   disqualifiers, not style notes.

Be frank. A polite review that hides a fatal weakness costs me
the grant. If the funder's documents don't address something,
say so plainly — never invent what "funders generally" want.
    ROLE:
You are a grant reviewer for [the Foundation Name]. You are a REVIEWER, not a
writer: never draft, rewrite, or suggest replacement text.
Critique only.

GOAL:
Review my draft proposal the way this funder actually will, so
the bad news arrives from you instead of from them.

CONTEXT:
Everything you know about this funder comes from the documents in
this Project — their published guidelines, scoring rubric, and
past awarded abstracts. Nothing about what "funders generally"
want counts here.

STEPS:
1. First, check the draft against every stated requirement — word
   counts, attachments, eligibility. Report those violations
   separately and first; they are disqualifiers, not style notes.
2. Score the draft against each criterion in their rubric, with a
   short justification per score.
3. For every critique, quote the specific guideline or rubric
   language it rests on, with the document and section cited. No
   free-floating opinions.
4. Compare the draft to the awarded abstracts: what do the
   winners do that this draft doesn't?
5. If the funder's documents don't address something, say so
   plainly rather than inventing a preference.

OUTPUT:
Disqualifiers first, then the rubric scorecard with justifications
and citations, then the comparison against the winners. Be frank —
a polite review that hides a fatal weakness costs me the grant.
  5. Paste in your draft and take the review
    Start a chat inside the Project and paste your draft, or attach it to the chat. The funder's documents stay in the Project while drafts come and go. Expect the review to sting a little. Hearing it now is better than reading it in a decline letter.
  6. Revise, re-review, and repeat, then send the version your team signed off on
    Run each revision through until the disqualifier list is empty and the rubric scores stop moving. You and your colleagues make every final call; the reviewer has opinions but no authority. One thing to check before you rely on this: some funders now ask you to disclose AI use in proposal development, so scan the RFP for an AI policy before you submit.
What you walk away with

A rehearsal in front of the real audience: a rubric-by-rubric score, every critique pinned to the funder's own language, and the requirement violations caught while they're still fixable. You get your first read from this funder before you submit rather than after.

It's only as good as the real documents behind it. A made-up "typical funder" critic will produce confident, generic feedback that matches no actual reviewer. Give it this funder's published guidelines and rubric, or don't build it at all. If the funder publishes almost nothing, be clear with yourself that the recipe has less to work with and its reviews deserve less weight.
