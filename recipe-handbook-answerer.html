Build an Expert · Ask

The handbook answerer

Your employee handbook, board bylaws, and fiscal policies — in one Project that answers "do I get bereavement leave for a grandparent?" with the section quoted. HR stops being the bottleneck for questions the documents already answer.

Nothing to flag — no sensitive data in, nothing published out
Information you give it

The PDFs you already have: the employee handbook, the board bylaws, the fiscal policies. You write nothing new — no summaries, no FAQ, no explanation of how your org works. The documents are the expertise; you're just making them answerable.

The recipe
  1. Round up the current versions
    Find the latest handbook, bylaws, and fiscal policies — not the 2019 draft in someone's Downloads folder. If you're not sure a version is current, this recipe just handed you a reason to find out.
  2. Create a Project named "Policy Answerer"
    In your AI chat, click Projects → New project. Add the version date to the name ("Policy Answerer — docs as of Jul 2026") so future-you knows how fresh it is.
  3. Upload the PDFs to Project knowledge
    Use Add content on the Project page and upload each document. Three or four files is typical; add the personnel-policy appendix and travel-reimbursement memo too if people actually ask about them.
  4. Paste these Project instructions
    Open the Project's Instructions box and paste this. The last paragraph is the one that keeps this tool in its lane.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Project instructions
    You answer staff questions about [our organization]'s policies
using ONLY the documents in this Project: [the employee handbook,
board bylaws, and fiscal policies].

Always quote the exact passage and cite the document name and
page/section you're drawing from, so the reader can verify it. If
the documents don't answer the question, say so plainly — never
fill the gap with a guess.

You know what these documents SAY, not what the organization has
since decided. If a question sounds like it involves an exception,
a judgment call, or a situation the policy doesn't cleanly cover,
answer what the document says AND add: "For your specific
situation, confirm with [the operations manager]." Anything
involving a dispute, accommodation, or personal circumstance goes
to a human, full stop.

End every answer with: "This is what the documents say — it is
not official HR guidance. Confirm with [name] before
acting on anything about leave, pay, or discipline."
  5. Test it with the questions you're sick of answering
    Ask the nine questions you field every month — bereavement leave, mileage rates, who signs checks over $5,000. Check each quoted section against the real PDF. When they all check out, you've got your answerer.
  6. Answer with it — then let people ask it themselves
    Start by using it yourself to reply faster. Once you trust it, walk a colleague through asking it directly. You've stopped being the bottleneck; you're now the editor of the documents it reads. Write one name next to this tool — whoever owns the handbook owns updating this Project the same week a policy changes. A stale-but-confident answerer is worse than none.
What you walk away with

A policy answerer that quotes the section, every time. The routine nine questions stop landing in your inbox — and when the answerer says "the handbook doesn't cover this," that's a signal the handbook needs a paragraph, which is useful too.

The expert knows what the document SAYS — not what the org has since DECIDED. If the board voted a new leave policy last month and the handbook hasn't been reissued, the answerer is confidently wrong. When documents and decisions conflict, the humans win. Two habits keep this safe: update the Project's files the same week a policy changes, and keep the "confirm with a human" line in the instructions.
