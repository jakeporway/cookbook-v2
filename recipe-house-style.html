The House Style, Bottled

Your five proudest pieces go into a Skill. Now everyone's drafts — the intern's, the board chair's, the ED's — come out sounding like one organization. Feed it a new best example whenever one exists, and it keeps getting more you.

Nothing to flag — no sensitive data in, nothing published out
Information you give it

Your 5 best published pieces — the ones you'd hold up and say "this is us" — plus 1 counter-example: something that made you wince, labeled "we never sound like this." You never write a style guide. The pieces are the style guide, demonstrated instead of described.

The recipe
  1. Gather your five proudest pieces — and the one that made you wince
    The newsletter people replied to. The appeal that worked. The annual report intro you'd frame. Then find one counter-example: jargon-heavy, stiff, off-key. You know it when you see it. Copy the text of all six somewhere handy.
  2. Create the skill and paste this in
    In your AI chat, make a new Project called House Style (If your AI tool offers "Skills" or custom instructions you can share, that's the same idea one step up — but a Project is all you need here.) and paste this as its instructions, with your six pieces where marked. That's the whole "teaching" — the examples do the work.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Prompt
    You are our organization's house-style editor. I'm teaching you
our voice from real examples — we will never write a style guide.

Below are [5] pieces we're proudest of, and 1 counter-example
labeled WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS.

When anyone on my team pastes a draft and says "house style,"
rewrite it so it sounds like the good examples — the same warmth,
sentence length, and way of talking about [the people we serve] —
and never like the counter-example. Keep every fact, name, and
number exactly as written; change only the voice. Return the
rewrite, then 2–3 short bullets on the biggest changes you made,
so we learn our own style over time.

When I say "new example," I'll paste a piece and tell you whether
it's a good one or a bad one — update how you work accordingly.

GOOD EXAMPLES:
[paste your 5 best pieces]

WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS:
[paste the 1 counter-example]
  3. Test it on a fresh draft
    Take something rough that's on your desk right now — a "please share this" paragraph, a program update — paste it in and say "house style." Read the rewrite. Does it sound like you on a good day? If it's off, say how ("too formal," "we'd never say 'utilize'") and it adjusts.
  4. Share it with the team
    Invite your colleagues to the Project, or show them where the skill lives. The instruction to them is one sentence: "Paste your draft, say 'house style,' read what comes back before you use it." Everyone you invite can see everything in the Project — the writing samples and the instructions — so include only what you'd share org-wide anyway.
  5. Feed it whenever a great (or terrible) piece appears
    Next time something lands beautifully — or lands with a thud — paste it in and say "new example: good one" or "new example: bad one." Thirty seconds, and the bottle gets a little more concentrated. This is the habit that makes it a living tool.
The counter-example is load-bearing. One "we never sound like this" teaches more than three more "like this" — it draws the line the good examples only gesture at. Don't skip it, and don't soften it: pick something that genuinely misses your voice, and say so plainly.
What you walk away with

One person's ear for the organization's voice, turned into something the whole team can use. The payoff isn't speed — it's consistency across people: the intern's first draft and the ED's board letter come out sounding like the same organization, and the comms lead stops being the bottleneck who rewrites everything.
