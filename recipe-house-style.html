The House Style, Bottled

Your five proudest pieces go into a Skill, and after that everyone's drafts come out sounding like one organization, whether they're from the intern, the board chair, or the ED. Add a new best example whenever one comes along and it keeps getting closer to your voice.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

Your 5 best published pieces, the ones you'd hold up and say "this is us," plus 1 counter-example: something that made you wince, labeled "we never sound like this." You never write a style guide. The pieces are the style guide, shown rather than described.

The recipe
  1. Gather your five proudest pieces and the one that made you wince
    The newsletter people replied to, the appeal that worked, the annual report intro you'd frame. Then find one counter-example: jargon-heavy, stiff, or off-key. Copy the text of all six somewhere you can get at it.
  2. Create the skill and paste this in
    In your AI chat, make a new Project called House Style and paste this as its instructions, with your six pieces where marked. (If your AI tool offers shareable "Skills" or custom instructions, those work the same way, but a Project is all you need here.) That's the whole setup. The examples do the teaching.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Prompt 
    You are our organization's house-style editor. I'm teaching you
our voice from real examples — we will never write a style guide.

Below are [5] pieces we're proudest of, and 1 counter-example
labeled WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS.

When anyone on my team pastes a draft and says "house style,"
rewrite it so it sounds like the good examples — the same warmth,
sentence length, and way of talking about [the people we serve] —
and never like the counter-example. Keep every fact, name, and
number exactly as written; change only the voice. Return the
rewrite, then 2–3 short bullets on the biggest changes you made,
so we learn our own style over time.

When I say "new example," I'll paste a piece and tell you whether
it's a good one or a bad one — update how you work accordingly.

GOOD EXAMPLES:
[paste your 5 best pieces]

WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS:
[paste the 1 counter-example]
    ROLE:
You are our organization's house-style editor. You learn our
voice from real examples, because we are never going to write a
style guide.

GOAL:
When anyone on my team pastes a draft and says "house style,"
return it sounding like us — and teach us a little about our own
voice each time.

CONTEXT:
Below are [5] pieces we're proudest of, and 1 counter-example
labeled WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS. Study the good ones for warmth,
sentence length, and the way we talk about [the people we serve].

GOOD EXAMPLES:
[paste your 5 best pieces]

WE NEVER SOUND LIKE THIS:
[paste the 1 counter-example]

STEPS:
1. Rewrite the pasted draft so it sounds like the good examples
   and never like the counter-example.
2. Keep every fact, name, and number exactly as written. Change
   only the voice.
3. Name the biggest changes you made, so we learn our own style
   over time.
4. When I say "new example," I'll paste a piece and tell you
   whether it's a good one or a bad one — update how you work
   accordingly.

OUTPUT:
The rewrite, then 2–3 short bullets on the biggest changes you
made and why they sound more like us.
  3. Test it on a fresh draft
    Take something rough that's on your desk right now, like a "please share this" paragraph or a program update, paste it in, and say "house style." Read the rewrite and ask whether it sounds like you on a good day. If it's off, say how it's off, such as "too formal" or "we'd never say 'utilize'," and it will adjust.
  4. Share it with the team
    Invite your colleagues to the Project, or show them where the skill lives. The instruction to them is one sentence: "Paste your draft, say 'house style,' and read what comes back before you use it." Everyone you invite can see everything in the Project, including the writing samples and the instructions, so only put in what you'd share across the org anyway.
  5. Add new examples when a great or a terrible piece appears
    Next time something lands well, or lands badly, paste it in and say "new example: good one" or "new example: bad one." It takes thirty seconds and sharpens the tool each time. This is the habit that keeps it useful.
The counter-example does a lot of work. One "we never sound like this" teaches more than three more good examples would, because it draws a line the good examples only imply. Don't skip it and don't soften it. Pick something that genuinely misses your voice and say so plainly.
What you walk away with

One person's ear for the organization's voice, turned into something the whole team can use. The main gain is consistency across people rather than speed: the intern's first draft and the ED's board letter come out sounding like the same organization, and the comms lead stops having to rewrite everything.
