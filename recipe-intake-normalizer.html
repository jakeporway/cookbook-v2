Reusable Skill · Normalize

The Intake Normalizer

Thirty partners, thirty formats — different columns, different date styles, different everything. One Skill takes whatever they send and returns it in your canonical format, flagging what it couldn't map instead of guessing silently.

Nothing to flag — no sensitive data in, nothing published out
Information you give it

Your canonical template — the exact columns, date format, and units you need — plus 3 real examples of the weird formats partners actually send (lightly fictionalized values are fine; headers plus a few rows each is plenty). You never write a mapping spec. The weird examples are the spec.

The recipe
  1. Gather your template and three genuinely weird partner files
    Your canonical format: headers plus one filled-in example row. Then pick the three formats that make you sigh — the one with dates written three ways, the one with merged headers, the one in a PDF. Headers and a few rows each is enough; swap in made-up values if the real ones are sensitive.
  2. Create the skill and paste this in
    In your AI chat, make a new Project called Intake Normalizer (If your AI tool offers "Skills" or custom instructions you can share, that's the same idea one step up — but a Project is all you need here.) and paste this as its instructions, with your examples where marked.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Prompt
    You are our intake normalizer. I'm teaching you our data format
from real examples.

Below is our CANONICAL TEMPLATE — the exact columns, date format,
and units we need. Then 3 real examples of the different formats
partners actually send us.

From now on, when anyone pastes or attaches a partner report and
says "normalize," return the data in our canonical format, as
[a table I can copy into a spreadsheet / a CSV file]. Map columns
by meaning, not name — ["lbs rescued," "pounds," and "weight (lb)"]
all belong in our [Pounds] column. Convert dates and units to
match the template exactly.

The most important rule: flag anything you couldn't confidently
map instead of guessing silently. End EVERY response with a
"COULDN'T MAP" list — the column, the value, and why — even when
the list is empty, so we know you checked.

When I say "new example," I'll paste a new weird format and show
you how it should have been mapped — update how you work
accordingly.

OUR CANONICAL TEMPLATE:
[paste your headers + one example row]

REAL FORMATS PARTNERS SEND:
[paste 3 examples — headers + a few rows each]
  3. Test it on a fresh partner file
    Take this month's most annoying report, paste it in, and say "normalize." Check two things: spot-check a handful of values against the original, and read the COULDN'T MAP list. If it flagged something you understand, tell it the answer — that's the skill learning. If it hands back a wall of text instead of a file, just say: "give me that as a downloadable CSV file."
  4. Share it with the team
    Invite the ops team to the Project. The routine is the same for everyone: paste the report, say "normalize," review the COULDN'T MAP list, copy the clean table out. Intake stops depending on who knows which partner's quirks.
  5. Feed it every new weird format
    When a partner sends something the skill fumbles, don't just fix it by hand — fix it once, then paste it back with "new example" and the correct mapping. Thirty partners means the first few months are training season; after that, the surprises get rare.
The COULDN'T MAP list is your human-in-the-loop — review it every time. An empty list means "I checked and it's clean," not "skip this step." A silent wrong guess in a data pipeline compounds: one mismapped column this month is a wrong annual total next year. The flag list is the whole reason this design is safe; reading it is the job.
What you walk away with

Thirty formats in, one format out — with a paper trail of what needed a human. The payoff isn't just the hours of retyping saved; it's consistency across people: the canonical file looks the same whoever ran intake, and the partner-quirk knowledge that used to live in one person's head now lives in a tool the whole team shares — and keeps teaching.
