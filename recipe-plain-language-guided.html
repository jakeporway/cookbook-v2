Say it so everyone can read it

Turn your program flyer or intake instructions into a 6th-grade reading level version, a large-print version, and a first-draft translation. The reading level is scored, and a fluent speaker reviews every translation before it goes out.

Nothing to flag. This is material you already hand out, and nothing goes out until a person has read it
What it will ask you

What the document is and who has to act on it. The clearest thing your org has ever published, which it uses as the target voice instead of asking you to describe one. Which language the translation should be in and where the readers are from. And who is going to read that translation before it goes out, which is the question that stops the recipe if you have no answer. You write nothing new, and you never have to name a reading level.

The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my research assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical. Your job is to run the following FOR me, asking me
only questions a non-technical person can answer, and being
strict about accuracy, because this is a document people read
to find out what they have to do to get something they need.

WHAT WE'RE DOING
Taking one document we hand to the people we serve, which
currently reads like the regulation it came from, and giving
me back three versions of it: a plain one at a 6th-grade
reading level with the score shown, a large-print-friendly
one laid out for big type, and a first-draft translation
marked as needing a human review. Every deadline, phone
number, address, and requirement survives all three
unchanged.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "what reading level and register should
  I target?" Instead ask "what's the clearest thing your org
  has ever published, a welcome flyer, a text-message
  reminder, whatever a real person understood on the first
  read? Paste it in" and take the voice and the reading level
  from that piece. If you genuinely can't infer something,
  give me 2-3 plain choices to pick from.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then work out how much
  text this chat will take in one message and whether I can
  attach a PDF here at all. If my document won't fit in one
  go, say so plainly now and tell me how we'll split it,
  rather than letting me discover it halfway through.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what I'll put the
  finished versions into, and hand them to me in a form that
  pastes straight in.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. What this document is, who has to act on it, and what they
   lose if they misread it. Then have me paste it in or
   attach it.
2. The clearest thing my org has ever published, in my own
   words first and then pasted in. If I can't think of one,
   offer me two or three kinds to go looking for, and if I
   still have nothing, say we'll go without it and you'll aim
   for plain and warm.
3. Which language the translation should be in, and where the
   readers are from, so the wording is the wording they
   actually use.
4. Who is going to read that translation before it goes out.
   If I don't have anyone, stop: go to the list at the end of
   this message and help me name a real person, because the
   translation does not go out without one.
5. What I'll put the finished versions into, and whether the
   large-print one gets printed or read on a screen.

HOW TO DO THE WORK (this part is for you, not me)
Accuracy outranks simplicity every time. Write three versions
of the document.
1. PLAIN: 6th-grade reading level or below. Short sentences,
   everyday words, "you" instead of "the applicant". Score it
   with Flesch-Kincaid, which is roughly the US school grade
   needed to read it, aim for 8 or below, 6 is great, and
   tell me the grade you reached.
2. LARGE-PRINT-FRIENDLY: the plain version restructured for
   big type. Short lines, one idea per chunk, clear headings,
   no dense paragraphs.
3. TRANSLATION, FIRST DRAFT: the plain version in the
   language I named, with "DRAFT, pending human review"
   across the top, and every term you weren't sure how to
   translate flagged underneath.
The hard rule throughout: every requirement, document name,
deadline, dollar amount, phone number, and address stays
exactly accurate. If making a sentence simpler would change
what somebody has to do, keep it precise and flag it for me
instead. Finish with a list of anything you kept complicated
on purpose. Give me your best draft of each version, not
endless variations.

AFTER THE THREE VERSIONS, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Your flagged list first: the sentences you kept
   complicated on purpose and the terms you weren't sure how
   to translate. Ask me about each one, since I'm the one who
   knows what the rule actually means.
2. The requirements check. Put every deadline, document name,
   dollar amount, phone number, and address from the original
   in one column with the plain version's wording beside it,
   and have me read across and confirm each pair. All of them
   matching means the plain version is safe to hand out. One
   mismatch means we check all three versions again, the
   translation included.
3. Getting the translation to the person I named: what to ask
   them for, how long to give them, and that their edits are
   part of this recipe rather than an optional extra.
4. Putting the three into use: the large-print one at 18pt or
   larger, the old version retired so nobody hands it out by
   mistake, and all three kept together so the next update
   covers all three.

RULES
- Never change what someone has to do in order to make a
  sentence simpler. Flag it and leave it precise.
- Never take "DRAFT, pending human review" off the
  translation, and never tell me it reads well enough to skip
  the reviewer.
- Best draft of each version, not a menu of variations.
- Before we finish, remind me out loud that the readability
  score is a guide and not a guarantee. The real test is one
  actual reader understanding it on the first read.

IF I HAVE NOBODY TO REVIEW THE TRANSLATION
Don't let me send it out anyway. Walk me through the options
in order: a bilingual colleague or board member, a partner
org that serves the same community, a community member paid a
stipend for an hour, a professional translator. Reviewing a
draft costs far less than translating from scratch, so tell
me that when I flinch at the last one. If I truly have nobody
today, tell me to hold the translation and publish the plain
and large-print versions on their own.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
do together, then ask your first question.
Machine translation is always a first draft. The assistant is instructed to mark it as a draft, to refuse to tell you it reads well enough to skip the reviewer, and to help you find a reviewer if you don't have one. Finding that person is still yours to do. A wrong word in intake instructions can cost someone a benefit.
The requirements check is yours to perform. The assistant lays the deadlines, phone numbers, and dollar amounts out side by side for you, but only you can confirm they're right, and only you know which ones matter most. Treat the reading-level score the same way: a guide, not a guarantee. The real test is whether an actual person understands it on the first read.
What you walk away with

Three accessible versions of a document people were struggling with: plain-language with a reading-level score, large-print, and a translation reviewed by a fluent speaker. You also have a repeatable process for every other confusing document your org hands out.
