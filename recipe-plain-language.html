Say it so everyone can read it

Information you give it

The current document, plus the plainest thing your org has ever published — a welcome flyer, a text-message script, whatever a real person actually understood on the first read. That artifact is the target register. Don't describe "simple and friendly"; show it.

The recipe
  1. Find your plainest published piece
    Dig up the clearest thing your org has ever put in front of the public. It doesn't have to be related to this document — it just has to sound the way you wish everything did.
  2. Open a normal chat
    Paste the current document in (or attach the PDF). No special mode.
  3. Paste this prompt with both documents dropped in
    The accuracy rule is the load-bearing line: plain language must never change what people actually have to do.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Prompt
    Here is a document we give to the people we serve. Right now it
reads like the regulation it summarizes:

[PASTE YOUR CURRENT DOCUMENT]

And here is the plainest thing our org has ever published — this
is the register I want, so match how it sounds:

[PASTE YOUR PLAINEST PAST PIECE]

Rewrite the document three ways:

1. PLAIN — 6th-grade reading level or below. Short sentences,
   everyday words, "you" instead of "the applicant". Score it
   (Flesch-Kincaid — a standard readability score; the number is
   roughly the U.S. school grade needed to read it. Aim for 8 or
   below; 6 is great) and tell me the grade level you reached.
2. LARGE-PRINT-FRIENDLY — the plain version restructured for big
   type: short lines, one idea per chunk, clear headings, no
   dense paragraphs.
3. SPANISH FIRST DRAFT — translate the plain version into
   [Spanish]. Mark it "DRAFT — pending human review" at the top.
   Flag any term you weren't sure how to translate.

The hard rule: every requirement, document name, deadline, phone
number, and address stays exactly accurate. If simplifying a
sentence would change what someone has to do, keep it precise and
flag it for me instead.

Give me your best draft of each. I'll pick and lightly edit —
don't offer endless variations.
  4. Check the requirements line by line
    Put the plain version next to the original and verify every deadline, document, and phone number survived. This is the ten minutes that makes the rest trustworthy.
  5. Send the translation to a fluent human
    A bilingual staff member, board member, or trusted volunteer reads the Spanish draft before anyone else does. Their edits are part of the recipe, not an optional extra. No bilingual staff? A partner org, a stipended community member, or a professional translator reviewing a draft (much cheaper than translating from scratch) all work.
  6. Format and put them into use
    Drop each version into your template — 18pt+ type for the large-print one — and retire the old letter. Keep all three files together so the next update touches all three.
What you walk away with

Three accessible versions of a document people were struggling with — plain-language (with a reading-level score), large-print, and human-reviewed Spanish — plus a repeatable move for every confusing document your org hands out.

Machine translation is a first draft — always. A fluent human reviews any translation before it reaches the people you serve; a wrong word in intake instructions can cost someone a benefit. And treat the reading-level score as a guide, not a guarantee: the real test is whether an actual person understands it on the first read.
