Understand what a rule change means for you

A proposed regulation or bill, read against your own program documents, so you know what actually changes for your organization and what doesn't. It will also draft a public comment if you decide to respond.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

The proposed rule as a PDF from the register or legislature site, plus your current program description or the policies it touches.

The recipe
  1. Get the actual text
    Use the PDF from the register or legislature site rather than a news story about it. Coverage summarizes, but the text is what governs you.
  2. Attach the rule and your program documents
    Start a new chat and attach both the proposed rule and the program description or policy it affects.
  3. Paste this prompt
    This is the comparison step: the rule against your own practice, rather than the rule in the abstract.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Chat prompt 
    Compare this proposed rule to my organization's current
practice as described in the attached documents. Give me:
1. Provisions that would require us to change something, with
   the rule section AND our document cited for each.
2. Provisions that sound alarming but don't apply to us, and why.
3. What the rule does NOT say that coverage might claim it says.
4. Open questions where our documents don't tell you enough
   about our practice — ask me rather than assume.

Be precise about proposed versus final, and about effective
dates.
    ROLE:
You are a policy analyst reading a proposed rule against how we
actually operate. You cite both sides of every comparison.

GOAL:
Tell me what this rule change would actually require of us —
separated from what the coverage says it requires.

CONTEXT:
The proposed rule is attached, along with documents describing my
organization's current practice. Be precise about proposed versus
final, and about effective dates — much of what gets circulated
about rules like this blurs both.

STEPS:
1. Read the proposed rule against our current practice as
   described in the attached documents.
2. Identify provisions that would require us to change something,
   citing the rule section AND our document for each.
3. Identify provisions that sound alarming but don't apply to us,
   and explain why not.
4. Identify what the rule does NOT say that coverage might claim
   it says.
5. Where our documents don't tell you enough about our practice,
   ask me rather than assume.

OUTPUT:
Those four sections in order, every point double-cited (rule
section + our document), with the open questions for me listed
last.
  4. Answer its open questions and re-run
    Fill in whatever it couldn't infer from your documents, then run the comparison again with that added.
  5. Verify the load-bearing citations in the original
    Check the sections that drive your biggest conclusions directly against the rule text.
  6. If responding, draft a grounded public comment
    Base the comment on the specific provisions and your own operational experience. Agencies read specific comments closely, while form letters are counted but rarely read. Someone with authority signs off before anything is submitted.
What you walk away with

A clear read on what actually changes for your organization, cited against both the rule and your own documents, plus a first draft of a public comment if you choose to submit one.

Rules change between proposal and final, and secondhand summaries are where nonprofits get caught out. Always give it the actual text. The "what it does NOT say" section is often the most useful part of the output, since a good deal of what circulates about a rule turns out not to be in it.
