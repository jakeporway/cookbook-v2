Understand what a rule change means for you

A proposed regulation or bill, read against your own program documents: what actually changes for your organization, what doesn't, and a first draft of a public comment if you choose to respond.

Information you give it

The proposed rule (PDF from the register or legislature site), plus your current program description or the policies it touches.

The recipe
  1. Get the actual text
    Not a news story about it — the PDF from the register or legislature site itself. Coverage summarizes; the text is what governs you.
  2. Attach the rule and your program documents
    Start a new chat and attach both the proposed rule and the program description or policy it affects.
  3. Paste this prompt
    This is the comparison step — the rule against your own practice, not the rule in the abstract.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Chat prompt
    Compare this proposed rule to my organization's current
practice as described in the attached documents. Give me:
1. Provisions that would require us to change something, with
   the rule section AND our document cited for each.
2. Provisions that sound alarming but don't apply to us, and why.
3. What the rule does NOT say that coverage might claim it says.
4. Open questions where our documents don't tell you enough
   about our practice — ask me rather than assume.

Be precise about proposed versus final, and about effective
dates.
  4. Answer its open questions and re-run
    Fill in whatever it couldn't infer from your documents, then run the comparison again with that added.
  5. Verify the load-bearing citations in the original
    Check the sections that drive your biggest conclusions directly against the rule text.
  6. If responding, draft a grounded public comment
    Base the comment on the specific provisions and your specific operational experience — agencies read specific comments; form letters get counted, not read. A human with authority signs off before anything is submitted.
What you walk away with

A clear read on what actually changes for your organization, cited against both the rule and your own documents — plus a grounded first draft of a public comment if you choose to submit one.

Rules change between proposal and final, and secondhand summaries are where nonprofits get burned. Always feed it the actual text. The "what it does NOT say" section is often the most valuable output — calm is a deliverable too.
