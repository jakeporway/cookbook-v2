Remix Your Content · Present

One story, a real deck

You wrote the quarterly report and the board meets Thursday. Turn the report you already finished into a real PowerPoint file, branded like last year's deck, editable by anyone, and ready to email the board chair tonight.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

The finished report or story, which is the content, plus last year's deck attached as the design template, with the instruction to make it look like that one. Don't write a paragraph describing your colors and fonts. The old deck is your brand in a way that adjectives never manage.

The recipe
  1. Pick the source content
    The quarterly report, the program story, or the annual letter: whatever is already written and finished. That becomes the deck's backbone, and you're not writing anything new.
  2. Dig up the deck your org already likes
    Last year's board deck, a program-review deck, or anything with your colors and layout already built in. It doesn't need to be recent. It needs to look like your organization.
  3. Attach both files and paste this prompt
    The report supplies the story and the old deck supplies the look. Swap the highlighted parts for your details.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Prompt 
    Attached are two files: my written report (the content) and a
slide deck we made before (the design to match).

Build me a PowerPoint deck (a real downloadable .pptx file) that
tells the report's story in [8-10] slides for [our board].

Match the attached deck's colors, fonts, and general layout.

Rules:
- Every number must come from the report, invent nothing.
- One idea per slide. Headlines a board member can read from
  the back of the room.
- End with [what we're asking the board for].
- If a chart would help, build it from the report's numbers and
  note which page they came from in the slide notes.
    ROLE:
You are building a presentation from a report someone else wrote.
You are a deck designer, not a co-author: every fact comes from
the report.

GOAL:
A real, downloadable .pptx deck that tells the report's story to
[our board] in [8-10] slides.

CONTEXT:
Attached are two files: my written report (the content) and a
slide deck we made before (the design to match). Match the
attached deck's colors, fonts, and general layout.

STEPS:
1. Find the report's actual argument first, then decide what
   earns a slide.
2. One idea per slide. Headlines a board member can read from the
   back of the room.
3. Every number must come from the report, invent nothing.
4. If a chart would help, build it from the report's numbers and
   note which page they came from in the slide notes.
5. End with [what we're asking the board for].

OUTPUT:
A downloadable .pptx file of [8-10] slides, on-brand, with source
pages in the slide notes wherever a figure appears.
  4. Open the .pptx and fix what a human should fix
    Open it in PowerPoint or Google Slides. The charts and layout usually land about 90% right, and the alignment fixes and final polish are yours to finish.
  5. Sanity-check every number against the source
    Check each slide against the original report line by line before it goes anywhere near the board. A wrong figure in front of your board does more damage than a plain slide ever will.
A real file beats a fancy one. An HTML slideshow doesn't work for a board deck, because nobody can edit it, forward it, or fix a typo on Thursday morning. Always ask for the .pptx.
If the file won't download, or arrives as plain text instead of a file, say exactly this back: "give it to me as a downloadable .pptx file."
What you walk away with

An editable, branded deck ready for Thursday, plus a technique you can reuse: providing a template by example. Next quarter it's the same two attachments and the same prompt with new numbers.
