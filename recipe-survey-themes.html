Three Hundred Survey Answers, Five Themes

Your post-program survey has one open-ended question and 300 answers nobody has read. Paste the column and get back the recurring themes, how often each appears, and verbatim quotes, along with a rule for checking those quotes before anyone gets quoted to a funder.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

One column from the survey export you already have: the answers to the open-ended question, copied straight out of the spreadsheet. If any answers name people, whether staff, participants, or sites, run the column through the redaction pass (Recipe 01) first.

The recipe
  1. Copy just the response column
    Open the survey export, click the column header for the open-ended question, and copy it. Leave the names, emails, and demographics columns behind, since the themes don't need them.
  2. Skim for names, and redact if you find any
    "Ms. Rivera was amazing" and "the guy at the Dorchester site" are both identifying. If answers mention people or places like that, run the column through the redaction pass before pasting. It takes two minutes and makes every step after this one safe.
  3. Paste this prompt with the responses inside it
    Swap in your actual survey question and your responses. If the chat says your message is too long, split the responses into two batches of about 150 and ask the same question twice, then ask it to merge the two theme lists.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Prompt 
    Below are all the responses to one open-ended question from our
post-program survey. The question was:
"What could we improve about the program?"

Read every response and give me:
1. The 4–6 themes that genuinely recur, named in plain language a
   board member would understand — no jargon.
2. How many responses touch each theme (one response can count
   toward more than one), roughly ranked by frequency.
3. Three VERBATIM quotes per theme — copied word for word, typos
   and all. Do not paraphrase, trim, or clean up grammar.
4. A short "didn't fit any theme" list, so nothing disappears
   silently.

Don't invent a theme to reach a rounder number. If something only
comes up two or three times but seems serious, call it out
separately rather than promoting it to a theme.

RESPONSES:
[paste the response column here]
    ROLE:
You are analyzing open-ended survey responses. You report what
respondents actually said, in their words where it counts.

GOAL:
Turn several hundred free-text answers into the handful of themes
that genuinely recur — without losing the outliers.

CONTEXT:
Below are all the responses to one open-ended question from our
post-program survey. The question was:
"What could we improve about the program?"

The audience for your summary is our board, so plain language
beats research vocabulary.

STEPS:
1. Read every response before naming a single theme.
2. Identify the 4–6 themes that genuinely recur, named in plain
   language a board member would understand — no jargon.
3. Count how many responses touch each theme (one response can
   count toward more than one) and rank roughly by frequency.
4. Pull three VERBATIM quotes per theme — copied word for word,
   typos and all. Do not paraphrase, trim, or clean up grammar.
5. Don't invent a theme to reach a rounder number. If something
   comes up only two or three times but seems serious, call it
   out separately rather than promoting it to a theme.

RESPONSES:
[paste the response column here]

OUTPUT:
The themes with counts and verbatim quotes, the separate
"serious but rare" callouts, and a short "didn't fit any theme"
list so nothing disappears silently.
  4. Spot-check the quotes against the source
    Search your spreadsheet with Ctrl+F or Cmd+F for a distinctive phrase from each quote and confirm it's there, word for word. The AI can occasionally blend two answers into a quote nobody actually wrote, and this two-minute check is what makes the quotes safe to use.
  5. Look more closely at the theme that matters most
    Ask a follow-up in the same chat, such as "show me every response that touched the scheduling theme." Now you're reading the 40 answers that matter rather than all 300.
  6. Save the prompt for the next survey cycle
    Two years of unread feedback took one afternoon. The next batch will take about ten minutes.
What you walk away with

A plain-language theme summary with counts, verified verbatim quotes ready for the board deck or funder report, and an "other" pile showing that nothing was quietly dropped. All of the feedback finally gets read.

Two rules before this leaves your desk. First, if answers name people, run the column through the redaction pass (Recipe 01) before pasting. Survey respondents didn't agree to have their words pasted into a chat. Second, spot-check every verbatim quote against the source spreadsheet before quoting anyone to a funder. A blended or misremembered quote in a funder report costs trust that's hard to rebuild.
