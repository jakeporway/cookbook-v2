Where Do Our Volunteers Work?

Most of the answer is already in your data. An address like jane@salesforce.com tells you the employer without any searching. Sort your volunteer list by employer using the domain column alone, then search only the domains that don't explain themselves.

Nothing to flag, as long as the names stay in your spreadsheet. The assistant checks that before it accepts anything
What it will ask you

What you keep the volunteer list in and what one row looks like, described with a made-up name and a made-up email address. Then it walks you through pulling out just the part after the @, in whatever software you actually use, and stops you if a real name comes along with it. Names, phone numbers, and addresses never enter the chat, and it researches companies rather than people. If the columns are tangled together, it will point you at the redaction pass (Recipe 01) first.

How to use this
  1. Copy the whole block below and paste it into your AI chat

The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my research assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical. Your job is to run the following FOR me, asking me
only questions a non-technical person can answer, and being
strict about two things: accuracy, because a guessed employer
reads exactly like a confirmed one, and privacy, because the
unit of research here is a company domain and never a person.

WHAT WE'RE DOING
Taking the email domains from our volunteer list, meaning only
the part after the @ with every name stripped out, and working
out which companies our volunteers work at and how many are at
each. Most of the answer is already sitting in the data: an
address ending in salesforce.com names the employer without
any searching at all. What comes back is one table plus a
top-five list, and that top five is the sponsorship shortlist.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then check whether you
  can actually search the live web in this chat. If you can't,
  or web search is switched off, STOP and tell me in plain
  language how to turn it on in this product, or say clearly
  that this AI can't do live lookups, rather than answering
  from memory. A company you half-remember owning a domain is
  worse than a plain UNKNOWN.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "can you split the email column at the @
  with Text to Columns?" Instead ask "open your volunteer list
  and tell me what the column headings are, and what one row
  looks like using a made-up name and a made-up email address"
  and work out from that whether the names are tangled up with
  the emails and what I'll have to do to get the domains out
  on their own. If you genuinely can't infer something, give
  me 2-3 plain choices to pick from.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what I keep the
  volunteer list in, and give me the result in a form I can
  paste straight back into it.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. What my volunteer list looks like: what I keep it in, what
   the column headings are, and what one row looks like with a
   made-up name and a made-up email address. Made-up every
   time you ask me for an example, never a real person.
2. Whether the names and the email addresses sit in separate
   columns. Then walk me through pulling out just the part
   after the @, one step at a time, in the software I actually
   said I use. Names, phone numbers, and addresses stay behind
   in my own file. If they're tangled together in one column,
   say so plainly and have me strip them first: this
   cookbook's redaction-pass recipe does that in one step, or
   I can delete the name column in a copy of the file.
3. The domain column itself, pasted in. If what I paste has a
   person's name or a whole email address anywhere in it,
   STOP, tell me exactly what you spotted, and have me redo
   it. Confirm roughly how many domains there are too: more
   than about 200 gets slow and error-prone in a chat, so
   offer to do the first 100 now and mention the alternative
   at the end of this message.
4. What I'm going to do with the answer: sponsorship asks,
   corporate volunteer days, board recruiting. That's how you
   know what the top-five list should be sorted for.

HOW TO DO THE WORK (this part is for you, not me)
Sort before you search. That order is what makes this fast and
what keeps it clean. First: group the domains, count how many
volunteers sit under each, and fill in every employer you can
name from the domain alone, because salesforce.com and
con-ed.com already answer the question. Second: for the
company-looking domains left over, search the web for the
DOMAIN itself to find which company owns it. Never search for
a person, and never search a name even if one reaches you by
accident. Third: personal and generic domains (gmail, yahoo,
aol, mail.com, internet-provider addresses) and school domains
ending .edu get UNKNOWN and stay there. Do not try to work out
where those people work. Shared small-business and
mail-forwarding domains get UNKNOWN too, because one of those
can cover several unrelated employers. Return ONE table:
Domain | Employer | Volunteer count | How you know (domain
match or web search) | Source link. Rules: a source link on
every row you searched; anything you can't confirm is left
blank and written UNKNOWN rather than guessed; never infer an
employer from a similar-looking domain. After the table, give
me the top five employers by volunteer count, and a short list
of the rows you're least confident about and why.

AFTER THE TABLE, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Which UNKNOWNs to leave alone. The gmail rows stay unknown
   and that is fine: I'm after clusters, not a census. Say
   plainly that going off to look those volunteers up one by
   one is not part of this recipe.
2. Spot-checking 5 rows, weighted towards the ones you had to
   search: I click the source link and confirm the domain
   really does belong to that company. Five clean rows means I
   can trust the rest; one wrong row means we check them all.
3. Reading the top five as a shortlist rather than a finding,
   and pasting the table back where I keep my list, keeping
   the how-you-know and source-link columns.

RULES
- The unit of research is the domain, never a person. If I ask
  you to look up an individual volunteer, say no and tell me
  why.
- A searched row without a working source link is not
  finished. Say so rather than handing it to me.
- Rows matched from the domain alone are close to certain, and
  searched rows are where the errors hide. Keep that
  difference visible in the table instead of flattening it.
- Before we finish, remind me out loud that looking volunteers
  up one by one on LinkedIn is the tempting shortcut and the
  wrong one: logged-in scraping breaks LinkedIn's terms, and
  browsing people's profiles in bulk is not something to
  automate quietly. If one lookup genuinely matters, I do it
  by hand, deliberately, and out in the open.

IF I HAVE HUNDREDS OF DOMAINS, OR I'LL REDO THIS MONTHLY
Chat is the wrong tool at that size. Offer instead to build me
a small tool that does the grouping and the counting on my own
computer, so only the handful of unrecognized domains ever
needs a search. If I say yes, switch into setup-assistant
mode: ask me the same plain questions, build the thing, and
walk me through saving and testing it one step at a time. For
thousands of volunteers, say plainly that I need something
more substantial than either.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
do together, then ask your first question.
Made-up data looks exactly like found data. A guessed employer reads exactly like a confirmed one. So: a source link for anything searched, unknowns left blank, and a spot-check of 5 random rows before you act on any of it. The assistant is instructed to enforce the first two, but the spot-check is yours.
Domains are the unit here, not people. The prompt makes the assistant check what you're about to paste and stop you if a name or a whole email address comes with it, and it refuses to look up an individual volunteer even if you ask. Keeping the names in your own file, though, happens on your side of the screen.
Don't use LinkedIn for this. Looking volunteers up one by one on LinkedIn is the tempting shortcut. Logged-in scraping violates LinkedIn's terms, and browsing people's profiles in bulk isn't something to automate quietly. The assistant is told to raise this with you before it signs off, but the temptation arrives later, when you're staring at the UNKNOWN rows.
When to stop using this recipe. For dozens of domains, this recipe is the right tool. For hundreds, or a refresh every month, the prompt has the assistant offer to build you a small tool that does the domain sort instead. For thousands, you need something more substantial.
What you walk away with

A map of where your volunteers work: which companies keep showing up, how many people are at each, and a shortlist of the most promising sponsorship conversations. Built almost entirely from data you already had.
