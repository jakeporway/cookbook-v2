Where Do Our Volunteers Work?

Most of the answer is already in your data. An address like jane@salesforce.com tells you the employer without any searching. Sort your volunteer list by employer using the domain column alone, then search only the domains that don't explain themselves.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

The email-domain column from the volunteer list you already have, meaning just the part after the @, with the names stripped out first. Recipe 01, the redaction pass, does this in one step, or you can simply delete the Name column in your copy and paste only the domain column.

The recipe
  1. Pull the domain column, drop the names
    In your spreadsheet, split the emails at the @ and copy only what comes after it. In Excel, that's select the column → Data → Text to Columns → Delimited → Other: @. If names and emails are tangled together, run the column through the redaction pass (Recipe 01) first, or simply delete the Name column in your copy and paste only the domain column.
  2. Paste the domains with this prompt
    Sort by domain first and search second. That order is what makes this efficient.

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Prompt 
    Here is the email-domain column from our volunteer list,
domains only, no names attached:

[paste your domain column, e.g. salesforce.com, gmail.com,
con-ed.com, nyu.edu…]

Step 1: sort by domain FIRST, before searching anything.
A domain like salesforce.com already tells you the employer.
Group the recognizable company domains and count how many
volunteers are at each.

Step 2: for company-looking domains you don't recognize,
search the web for the DOMAIN itself (never a person) to
confirm which company it belongs to.

Step 3: personal and generic domains (gmail, yahoo, aol,
ISPs) and school domains (.edu): mark the employer UNKNOWN.
Do not try to look those people up.

Return a table: Domain | Employer | Volunteer count | How you
know (domain match / web search) | Source link for anything
you searched. If you can't confirm a domain, leave it blank
and write UNKNOWN, do not guess. Then list the top 5
employers by volunteer count.
    ROLE:
You are doing employer research from email domains only. You
research domains, never people.

GOAL:
Find out where our volunteers work in aggregate, so we know which
companies we already have a foothold in.

CONTEXT:
Here is the email-domain column from our volunteer list, domains
only, no names attached:

[paste your domain column, e.g. salesforce.com,
gmail.com, con-ed.com, nyu.edu…]

Never search for an individual. The unit of research is the
domain.

STEPS:
1. Sort by domain FIRST, before searching anything. A domain like
   salesforce.com already tells you the employer. Group the
   recognizable company domains and count how many volunteers are
   at each.
2. For company-looking domains you don't recognize, search the
   web for the DOMAIN itself to confirm which company it belongs
   to.
3. Personal and generic domains (gmail, yahoo, aol, ISPs) and
   school domains (.edu): mark the employer UNKNOWN. Do not try
   to look those people up.
4. If you can't confirm a domain, leave it blank and write
   UNKNOWN, do not guess.

OUTPUT:
A table: Domain | Employer | Volunteer count | How you know
(domain match / web search) | Source link for anything you
searched.

Then list the top 5 employers by volunteer count.
  3. Read the top-5 list, which is your sponsorship shortlist
    Eight volunteers at one company is a genuine opening. That cluster is the conversation your partnerships lead has been looking for.
  4. Spot-check 5 rows, especially the searched ones
    Click each source link and confirm the domain really belongs to that company. Rows matched by domain are close to certain, while searched rows are where errors hide. Watch for generic domains like mail.com and ISP addresses, along with shared small-business domains, all of which can mislead. Treat those as UNKNOWN.
  5. Leave the UNKNOWNs unknown
    Gmail addresses don't reveal employers, and that's fine, because you're looking for clusters rather than a complete census. Don't go looking those people up online. See the warning below.
What you walk away with

A map of where your volunteers work: which companies keep showing up, how many people are at each, and a shortlist of the most promising sponsorship conversations. Built almost entirely from data you already had.

Made-up data looks exactly like found data. A guessed employer reads exactly like a confirmed one. So: a source link for anything searched, unknowns left blank, and a spot-check of 5 random rows before you act on any of it.
When to stop using this recipe. For dozens of domains, this recipe is the right tool. For hundreds, or a refresh every month, ask the AI to build a small tool that does the domain sort automatically. For thousands, you need something more substantial.
Don't use LinkedIn for this. Looking volunteers up one by one on LinkedIn is the tempting shortcut. Logged-in scraping violates LinkedIn's terms, and browsing people's profiles in bulk isn't something to automate quietly. If one specific lookup genuinely matters, do it deliberately, by hand, and openly, rather than as a batch job.
