Where Do Our Volunteers Work?

Before searching anything: their email domains already told you. jane@salesforce.com is not a mystery. Sort your volunteer list by employer from the domain column alone, then search only the domains that don't explain themselves.

Nothing to flag — no sensitive data in, nothing published out
Information you give it

The email-domain column from the volunteer list you already have — just the part after the @, with the names stripped out first (Recipe 01, the redaction pass, does this in one step) — or simpler: delete the Name column in your copy and paste only the domain column.

The recipe
  1. Pull the domain column, drop the names
    In your spreadsheet, split emails at the @ and copy only what's after it. (In Excel: select the column → Data → Text to Columns → Delimited → Other: @.) If names and emails are tangled together, run the column through the redaction pass (Recipe 01) first — or simpler: delete the Name column in your copy and paste only the domain column.
  2. Paste the domains with this prompt
    Domain-sort first, search second — that order is the whole trick.

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Prompt
    Here is the email-domain column from our volunteer list —
domains only, no names attached:

[paste your domain column, e.g. salesforce.com, gmail.com,
con-ed.com, nyu.edu…]

Step 1 — sort by domain FIRST, before searching anything.
A domain like salesforce.com already tells you the employer.
Group the recognizable company domains and count how many
volunteers are at each.

Step 2 — for company-looking domains you don't recognize,
search the web for the DOMAIN itself (never a person) to
confirm which company it belongs to.

Step 3 — personal and generic domains (gmail, yahoo, aol,
ISPs) and school domains (.edu): mark the employer UNKNOWN.
Do not try to look those people up.

Return a table: Domain | Employer | Volunteer count | How you
know (domain match / web search) | Source link for anything
you searched. If you can't confirm a domain, leave it blank
and write UNKNOWN — do not guess. Then list the top 5
employers by volunteer count.
  3. Read the top-5 list — that's your sponsorship shortlist
    Eight volunteers at one company is a warm door. That cluster is the conversation your partnerships lead has been looking for.
  4. Spot-check 5 rows, especially the searched ones
    Click each source link and confirm the domain really belongs to that company. Domain-match rows are near-certain; searched rows are where errors hide. One catch: generic domains (mail.com, ISP addresses) and shared small-business domains can mislead — treat those as UNKNOWN, not near-certain.
  5. Leave the UNKNOWNs unknown
    Gmail addresses don't reveal employers, and that's fine — you're looking for clusters, not a complete census. Resist the urge to go find those people online (see the red zone below).
What you walk away with

An employer-cluster map of your volunteer base — which companies keep showing up, how many people at each, and a shortlist of the warmest sponsorship conversations. Built almost entirely from data you already held.

Made-up data looks exactly like found data. A guessed employer reads exactly like a confirmed one. The rule: a source link for anything searched, unknowns stay blank, and spot-check 5 random rows before you act on any of it.
The lookup line. Dozens of domains → this recipe. Hundreds, or a refresh every month → ask the AI to build a small tool that does the domain sort automatically. Thousands → you've outgrown the recipe.
LinkedIn is the red zone. Looking volunteers up one by one on LinkedIn is the tempting shortcut — don't. Logged-in scraping violates LinkedIn's terms, and batch-browsing people's profiles isn't something to automate quietly. If a specific lookup ever matters that much, make it a deliberate, disclosed, by-hand choice — never a batch job.
