Turn Whiteboard Photos Into a Real Document

The flip-charts and whiteboards from your retreat, photographed and handed over, come back as a structured document — themes, decisions, owners, parking lot — before everyone forgets what the arrows meant.

Information you give it

Photos of each board, plus one line of context per board — for example, "this one is from the fundraising breakout." That one line does more work than you'd expect.

The recipe
  1. Photograph every board before anyone erases it
    Straight-on, whole board in frame. This is the habit that makes the whole recipe possible — a half-erased board can't be reconstructed by anyone, AI or human.
  2. Attach the photos with a one-line label for each
    "Board 1 — fundraising breakout," "Board 2 — full group wrap-up," and so on.
  3. Paste this prompt

    You don't need to understand every word below — it's instructions for the AI, not for you. Swap the [bracketed] parts; copy the rest exactly as-is.

    Prompt
    Transcribe everything on these whiteboard photos. Keep each board
separate, using the labels I've given them: [Board 1 — fundraising
breakout, Board 2 — full group wrap-up, ...]. Preserve lists and
groupings as they appear.

Where arrows or circles connect items, describe the connection in
words rather than just noting that a line exists.

Then organize everything into:
1. Themes discussed
2. Decisions made
3. Action items, with an owner if one is written down
4. Open questions (parking lot)

Mark anything you cannot read as [UNREADABLE], and anything you
inferred — like what an arrow or circle means — as [INFERRED], so I
can check it.
  4. Review it with one other person who was in the room
    A co-reader check. Two people who were actually there catch a wrong [INFERRED] connection far faster than one person working alone.
  5. Share the document while memories are fresh
    Send it to the team the same day if you can. A week later, the arrows are just as hard for humans to remember as they were for the model.
What you walk away with

A structured retreat document — themes, decisions, owners, and an open-questions list — checked by someone who was in the room, sent out before anyone's memory of the arrows fades.

The transcription is trustworthy; the interpretation of arrows and circles is a guess wearing a label. That's exactly why [INFERRED] items get checked by someone who was there. And photograph before erasing — it's the one habit that makes this recipe possible at all.

Helps you handle bad handwriting and internal shorthand.

How did the boards turn out — anything that's going to be hard to read back?
Our handwriting is terrible and half of it is in shorthand only our team understands.
Add a short glossary line to the prompt — something like "MA = Merit America, PD = program director." The one-line context labels already do more than you'd think; a glossary line does the rest. It won't fix genuinely illegible handwriting, but it clears up shorthand that reads as gibberish to anyone outside your team.
