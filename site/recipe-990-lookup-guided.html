Size Up Any Nonprofit in 30 Seconds

Paste a list of org names and get back each one's budget size, staff count, and program focus from their public 990 filings. It's a fast way to narrow a list of 60 possible partners down to the 15 worth calling first.

Nothing to flag. Everything here is public record, and nothing gets published
What it will ask you

Where your list came from, which is how it works out whether you already have tax ID numbers or just names. What you're deciding with it. Whether the orgs are all US nonprofits. And then the list itself, pasted in as it is. You don't need to know what an EIN is, you don't need to clean the list up first, and you don't need to check whether your AI can search the web: it checks that itself and stops if it can't.

How to use this
  1. Copy the whole block below and paste it into your AI chat
    Have your list of org names somewhere you can copy from. Nothing to fill in and nothing to clean up first. The assistant makes sure your AI can actually search the live web before it starts, does the lookups against the public filings, and then walks you through resolving the name collisions and spot-checking the numbers.
The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my research assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical. Your job is to run the following lookup FOR me,
asking me only questions a non-technical person can answer,
and being strict about accuracy, because a made-up budget
figure reads exactly as plausibly as a real one.

WHAT WE'RE DOING
Taking my list of nonprofit names and finding, for each one,
how big it is, how many people it employs, and what it
actually does, all from its most recent public tax filing,
with a link on every row to the filing the numbers came from.
The point is to turn a long list of maybe-partners into a
short list worth calling first.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then check whether you
  can actually search the live web in this chat. If you
  can't, or web search is switched off, STOP and tell me in
  plain language how to turn it on in this product, or say
  clearly that this AI can't do live lookups, rather than
  answering from memory. Budgets and staff counts from memory
  are worse than no answer at all.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "does your list include EINs?" Instead
  ask "where did this list come from: a coalition roster, a
  conference program, a funder's list of grantees?" and work
  out from that whether it already carries tax ID numbers and
  cities or is just names. If you genuinely can't infer
  something, give me 2-3 plain choices to pick from.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what I keep this
  list in, and give me the result in a form I can paste
  straight back into it.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. Where my list came from: a coalition roster, a conference
   program, a funder's grantee page, something I typed up.
2. What I'm deciding with it, so you know what to sort by:
   who to call first, who is our size, who does the work
   closest to ours.
3. Whether these are all US nonprofits. Tax filings only
   exist for US filers, so tell me plainly if something on my
   list won't be findable this way, and don't quietly return
   a blank row instead.
4. My list itself: have me paste it in as it is, and confirm
   roughly how many orgs there are. If it's more than about
   50, warn me this gets slow and error-prone in chat, offer
   to do the first 25 now, and mention the built-tool
   alternative at the end of this message.
5. What I keep the list in, so the table comes back in a
   shape I can paste in without retyping.

HOW TO DO THE LOOKUP (this part is for you, not me)
Work only from primary sources: the filings themselves, never
a third-party write-up of them. ProPublica's Nonprofit
Explorer (projects.propublica.org/nonprofits) carries every
Form 990 filing, free, and is your first stop. For each org,
find the most recent public Form 990 and record: the tax ID,
the filing year, total revenue, total expenses, number of
employees, and a one-line description of what the org does,
taken from the mission statement in the filing itself. Return
ONE table: Org | Tax ID | Filing year | Total revenue | Total
expenses | Employees | What they do | Source. Rules: a source
link in every row, pointing at that org's filing or its
Nonprofit Explorer page; if you can't find an org or a
figure, leave the cell blank and write UNKNOWN rather than
guessing, estimating, or borrowing the number from a
different year; where a name matches more than one nonprofit,
do NOT choose, flag it and list the candidates with their
cities so I can pick. Below the table, give me three short
lists: names that matched more than one org, rows with
anything UNKNOWN, and the rows you're least confident about
and why.

AFTER THE TABLE, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Resolving the collisions and the UNKNOWNs. For a name
   collision, I open both source links and pick mine by city,
   tell you which, and you correct the row. There are usually
   only a few.
2. Spot-checking 5 random rows: I click the source link and
   confirm that the revenue figure and the filing year on
   that page match what's in my table. Five clean rows means
   I can trust the rest; one wrong row means we check them
   all.
3. Sorting the table the way I said I needed it, or grouping
   the orgs into rough size bands, and pasting the result
   back where I keep my list, keeping the source-link and
   filing-year columns.

RULES
- A row without a working source link is not finished. Say so
  rather than shipping it.
- Never estimate a figure, and never carry a number over from
  an older filing to fill a gap in a newer one.
- Never pick between two nonprofits with the same name on my
  behalf. That is my call, and I have context you don't.
- Before we finish, remind me out loud that these filings run
  one to two years behind, so the "current" budget I quote in
  a meeting may be a two-year-old picture, and I should say
  the filing year out loud whenever I cite a number.

IF MY LIST RUNS TO HUNDREDS OF ORGS, OR I'LL REDO IT MONTHLY
Chat is the wrong tool at that size. Offer instead to build
me a small tool that pulls from ProPublica's free Nonprofit
Explorer data directly, so the whole list refreshes in one
go. If I say yes, switch into setup-assistant mode: ask me
the same plain questions, build the thing, and walk me
through saving and testing it one step at a time. If we're
talking about thousands of orgs, or anything the
organization's finances depend on, say plainly that I need a
real data service instead.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
do together, then ask your first question.
Made-up data looks exactly like found data. An invented revenue figure reads just as plausibly as a real one. So: a source link in every row, unknowns left blank, and a spot-check of 5 random rows against the filings before you act on any of it. The assistant is instructed to enforce the first two, but the spot-check is yours.
990s run one to two years behind. The "current" budget you quote in a meeting may be two years old. The prompt makes the assistant put the filing year in the table and remind you before it signs off, but saying that year out loud in the meeting is on you.
When to stop using this recipe. For dozens of orgs, this recipe is the right tool. For hundreds, or a refresh every month, the prompt has the assistant offer to build you a small tool that pulls the data directly instead. For thousands, or anything business-critical, you need a real data service.
What you walk away with

A one-screen table with budget size, staff count, and program focus for every org on your list, each row linked to the filing it came from. Work that used to mean opening sixty PDFs now takes about half a minute per org.
