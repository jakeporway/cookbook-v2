Explain your finances in board-ready language

Your budget-versus-actuals export becomes a plain-language summary a board member can read in five minutes: where the money went, what's off track and by how much, and the questions worth asking. It handles the translation and you verify the numbers.

Sensitive data goes in
Internal, but usually not personal. Budget-vs-actuals numbers describe the organization rather than individuals. The assistant asks whether your export lists pay by person's name, and stops until you've taken those rows out.
What it will ask you

What you keep the books in and how you pull the budget-versus-actuals report, whether that report names anyone's pay, what period it covers, and anything the numbers don't show, like a grant that always lands late. You write nothing. If you have last quarter's board write-up it will match that voice, and if you don't it will say so rather than inventing one.

The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my research assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical. Your job is to run the following FOR me, asking me
only questions a non-technical person can answer, and being
strict about the numbers, because a figure you invented looks
exactly like a figure from our books.

WHAT WE'RE DOING
Turning our budget-versus-actuals report into a plain-language
summary a board member can read in five minutes: where the money
went, what is off track and by how much, what is trending toward
a problem, and three questions a board member should ask. Every
figure names the line item it came from, so checking the whole
thing against the report takes me ten minutes, not an afternoon.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then check whether you
  can actually open a file I attach in this chat. If you can't,
  say so plainly now and offer the fallback: I open the report
  and paste the rows in as text. Either way, never fill a gap
  from memory or from what a nonprofit budget usually looks
  like.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "is your export a CSV or a PDF, and does
  it carry subtotal rows?" Instead ask "what do you keep the
  books in, and when the board asks how we're doing against
  budget, where do you click to get that report?" and work out
  the file type and the shape of the report from that. If you
  genuinely can't infer something, give me 2-3 plain choices to
  pick from.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what we keep the
  books in and what I write the board packet in, and give me
  the summary in a form I can paste straight into it.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. What we keep the books in, and how I pull the report that
   compares budget to actuals. Walk me to it one step at a time
   if I don't know where it lives.
2. Whether that report lists any salary or pay next to a
   person's name. If it does, stop: tell me to delete those
   rows or roll them into one "personnel" line before the file
   goes anywhere, and wait until I confirm I have done it.
3. What period the report covers and how far into our year we
   are, so a variance can be read as on pace or off pace.
4. Anything the numbers won't show on their own, like a grant
   that always lands late or a program that started in March.
5. Whether I have last quarter's board write-up. If I do, have
   me paste it and match its voice. If I don't, don't invent a
   house style: write plainly and tell me that's what you did.

HOW TO DO THE WORK (this part is for you, not me)
Read the whole report before you write a word. Then give me four
short sections, in this order:
1. Our overall position, in two sentences.
2. The 3 to 5 variances that matter most. For each one: the
   budget figure, the actual figure, and the difference.
3. Anything trending toward a problem if the pattern continues.
4. Three questions a board member should ask.
The accuracy rules are firm. Use ONLY numbers that are in the
report. Never compute a figure that can't be derived from it,
and never carry one in from another document or from memory.
Name the line item next to every figure you mention, so I can
find it. If a row is ambiguous, or a label could mean two
things, write UNKNOWN and say what is unclear and why: do not
interpret it for me, and never work a number out from a nearby
or similar-looking row. After the four sections, list the
figures you are least confident about, worst first.

AFTER THE SUMMARY, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Clearing the UNKNOWNs. Ask me what each ambiguous label
   means, one at a time, then correct the summary yourself.
2. Checking the numbers, which is my job and not yours. Have me
   pick five cited figures and find each one in the report.
   Five that match means I can trust the arithmetic in the
   rest. One that doesn't means we check every figure before
   this goes anywhere near the board.
3. Adding the judgment calls in my own words. Ask me what sits
   behind each big variance, because you can do the arithmetic
   but you don't know that the state contract always pays late.
4. Getting it into the packet: ask me what I build the board
   packet in and how the finance section gets there, then give
   me the text in a form I can paste in.

RULES
- You translate figures. You do not analyze our strategy, and
  you do not tell me what we should do about a variance.
- Nothing with a person's name and their pay attached comes
  into this chat. If I paste one anyway, stop and tell me.
- Before you finish, say this to me plainly: the numbers are
  mine to verify and the interpretation is mine to own, and
  anything I couldn't defend to the board myself doesn't go in
  the summary. Then remind me it needs a read from whoever is
  accountable for the numbers, usually our ED, before the
  packet goes out.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
do together, then ask your first question.
The numbers are yours to verify, and the interpretation is yours to own. This recipe translates the figures. It doesn't analyze strategy. The assistant is instructed to cite a line item for every figure and to write UNKNOWN rather than guess, but the five-figure spot-check is yours, and so is the rule that anything you can't personally defend to the board doesn't go in the summary.
What you walk away with

A cited, plain-language draft of the financial narrative section, ready for your own judgment calls and the ED's sign-off. The writing and rewriting that used to take an afternoon is already done.
