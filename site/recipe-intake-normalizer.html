Reusable Skill · Normalize

The Intake Normalizer

Thirty partners send you thirty formats, with different columns, different date styles, and different conventions throughout. One Skill takes whatever they send and returns it in your canonical format, flagging what it couldn't map rather than guessing silently.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
Information you give it

Your canonical template, meaning the exact columns, date format, and units you need, plus 3 real examples of the formats partners actually send. Lightly fictionalized values are fine, and headers plus a few rows each is plenty. You never write a mapping spec. The examples are the spec.

The recipe
  1. Gather your template and three genuinely awkward partner files
    Start with your canonical format: headers plus one filled-in example row. Then pick the three formats that give you the most trouble, such as the one with dates written three ways, the one with merged headers, and the one that arrives as a PDF. Headers and a few rows each is enough, and you can swap in made-up values if the real ones are sensitive.
  2. Create the skill and paste this in
    In your AI chat, make a new Project called Intake Normalizer and paste this as its instructions, with your examples where marked. (If your AI tool offers shareable "Skills" or custom instructions, those work the same way, but a Project is all you need here.)

    You don't need to understand every word below. It's written for the AI to read, not for you. Swap in the [bracketed] parts and copy the rest exactly as it is.

    Prompt 
    You are our intake normalizer. I'm teaching you our data format
from real examples.

Below is our CANONICAL TEMPLATE: the exact columns, date format,
and units we need. Then 3 real examples of the different formats
partners actually send us.

From now on, when anyone pastes or attaches a partner report and
says "normalize," return the data in our canonical format, as
[a table I can copy into a spreadsheet / a CSV file]. Map columns
by meaning, not name: ["lbs rescued," "pounds," and "weight (lb)"]
all belong in our [Pounds] column. Convert dates and units to
match the template exactly.

The most important rule: flag anything you couldn't confidently
map instead of guessing silently. End EVERY response with a
"COULDN'T MAP" list: the column, the value, and why, even when
the list is empty, so we know you checked.

When I say "new example," I'll paste a new weird format and show
you how it should have been mapped, update how you work
accordingly.

OUR CANONICAL TEMPLATE:
[paste your headers + one example row]

REAL FORMATS PARTNERS SEND:
[paste 3 examples, headers + a few rows each]

1. Map columns by meaning, not name: ["lbs rescued," "pounds," and "weight (lb)"] all belong in
   our [Pounds] column.
2. Convert dates and units to match the template exactly.
3. Flag anything you couldn't confidently map instead of guessing

   accordingly.

Then end EVERY response with a "COULDN'T MAP" list: the column,
the value, and why, even when the list is empty, so we know you
checked.
  3. Test it on a fresh partner file
    Take this month's most awkward report, paste it in, and say "normalize." Check two things: spot-check a handful of values against the original, and read the COULDN'T MAP list. If it flagged something you know the answer to, tell it, and it will handle that case next time. If it hands back a wall of text instead of a file, say "give me that as a downloadable CSV file."
  4. Share it with the team
    Invite the ops team to the Project. The routine is the same for everyone: paste the report, say "normalize," review the COULDN'T MAP list, and copy the clean table out. Intake stops depending on who happens to know which partner's quirks.
  5. Add every new format it fumbles
    When a partner sends something the skill fumbles, fix it once and then paste it back with "new example" and the correct mapping. With thirty partners, the first few months are mostly training. After that the surprises get rare.
The COULDN'T MAP list is where you come in, so review it every time. An empty list means "I checked and it's clean," not "you can skip this step." A silent wrong guess compounds: one mismapped column this month becomes a wrong annual total next year. The flag list is what makes this design safe, and reading it is part of the work.
What you walk away with

Thirty formats in, one format out, with a record of everything that needed a person's judgment. Beyond the hours of retyping saved, you get consistency across people: the canonical file looks the same whoever ran intake, and the knowledge of each partner's quirks now sits in a tool the whole team shares and keeps adding to.
