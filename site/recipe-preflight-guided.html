Set up your AI the right way

A ten-minute check before anything else: which plan you're on, whether web search is turned on, what your privacy and training settings say, and where Projects live. Half the problems people hit with these recipes come back to setup.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
What it will ask you

Whether this account is yours or one your organization set up, what your team is likely to paste into it, roughly what you want AI for, and where your colleagues would genuinely see a one-page record. Then it reads the four settings screens with you, one at a time. You prepare nothing. It is told to say "I can't see that from here" and send you to the screen rather than guess, which is the whole point of this recipe.

The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my setup assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical, and I don't want to become technical today. Your job
is to set the following up FOR me, asking me only questions a
non-technical person can answer, and to be strict about what
you actually know, because a confident guess about a privacy
setting is worse than no answer at all.

WHAT WE'RE MAKING
A written record of how our AI is actually set up: which plan
we're on, whether our conversations can be used to train
models, whether web search is turned on, and where the reusable
workspaces live. Four checks, run once with you now, then
written on one page my colleagues can see. Until we have a
fuller policy, that page is our policy. And a habit: the same
four checks again whenever something changes.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then adapt every screen
  name and menu path that follows to THAT product. If you
  can't see something about my account, say "I can't see that
  from here" and tell me which screen to open instead. Never
  fill it in from what that product usually defaults to.
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask me which subscription tier I'm on or
  what my data retention policy says. Instead ask "does your
  organization pay for this, and who set it up, you or someone
  in IT?" and work out from that whether I'm on a personal
  account or a managed work one, which decides where these
  settings live and whether I'm even allowed to change them.
  If you genuinely can't infer something, give me 2-3 plain
  choices to pick from.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me where my team
  actually keeps things everyone reads, and put the record
  there rather than somewhere that suits you.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that. What I read
  off the screen always beats what you expected to be there.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. Whether this account is mine personally or one my
   organization set up, and who I'd ask if a setting turns out
   to be locked.
2. What my team is likely to put into this tool: only public
   material, our own internal working documents, or anything
   about the people we serve. If it's the third, say so
   plainly: the training and privacy answer stops being a
   formality and has to be settled before anyone pastes
   anything real.
3. Roughly what I want to use AI for, so you know whether I
   actually need web search turned on and whether I need the
   reusable workspaces at all.
4. Where my colleagues would genuinely see a one-page record:
   a shared doc, the staff handbook, a pinned message, a
   printed note on the wall.

WHAT TO SET UP (this part is for you, not me)
Two things: the four checks, and the record I keep afterwards.

Walk me through the checks ONE screen at a time, using the
names my product uses, and ask me to read back what I actually
see before you move on:

1. Plan. Which plan I'm on and what it limits: how much I can
   use in a day, and which features are missing from it.
2. Training and privacy. Whether conversations here can be
   used to train models, where that setting lives, what it is
   set to right now, and what I should set it to given my
   answer about what my team will paste in. If it's a managed
   work account, tell me it may be locked and that the person
   who set it up is the one who can change it.
3. Web search. Whether this product can look things up on the
   live web, whether it's switched on for me, and how I can
   tell from an answer whether it searched or not.
4. Reusable workspaces, the place where saved instructions and
   uploaded files live, called Projects, GPTs, Gems, or Agents
   depending on the product. Have me open one, find the
   instructions box and the place files are added, then close
   it. I only need to know where they are.

Then give me the record to save, as a form I fill in with you,
so I can re-check and re-date the same form later rather than
rewriting it:

  "HOW OUR AI IS SET UP, checked [DATE]
  Product and plan: [PLAN]
  Who can change these settings: [WHO]
  Can our conversations train models: [YES/NO/UNKNOWN],
  setting found under [WHERE]
  Web search: [ON/OFF/NOT AVAILABLE]
  Reusable workspaces: [WHERE THEY LIVE]
  What we will and won't paste in: [WHAT]
  Check again when: our plan changes, someone new starts
  using AI here, the provider emails about a policy change,
  or six months goes by, whichever comes first."

AFTER IT'S SET UP, WALK ME THROUGH
1. A test of you, not of me: ask me to ask you what plan I'm
   on, then compare your answer to what I just read on the
   settings screen. If you couldn't tell, say so. That's the
   lesson, and it's a pass, not a failure.
2. Filling in the record with me, one line at a time, reading
   it back to me at the end. Any line still saying UNKNOWN is
   a to-do with a name on it, not a blank to leave.
3. Putting the record where my colleagues will see it, and
   telling one other person at my org that it exists.
4. The habit: the four triggers for running these checks
   again, and who runs them.

RULES
- Never state my plan, my privacy setting, or my search status
  as fact unless you can actually see it. Say "I can't see
  that from here", name the screen I should open, and wait for
  me to read it back. Do not infer any of it from the usual
  default.
- Settings screens move and plans get renamed. Describe what
  I'm looking for in plain words rather than promising an
  exact menu path, and if what I'm seeing doesn't match, work
  from what I describe.
- Before we finish, tell me this out loud: a setup nobody
  rechecks goes quietly wrong. Providers change their defaults
  and their plans, and the page on my wall will keep saying
  what was true the day we wrote it.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
set up together, then ask your first question.
Settings screens move, and plans get renamed. The prompt tells the assistant to describe what you're looking for rather than promise an exact menu path, and to work from what you describe when the screen doesn't match. Whichever assistant your org uses, the same four checks exist under different names.
Your AI often cannot see its own account settings. The prompt forbids it from filling that in from the usual default, so expect to hear "I can't see that from here, open this screen" at least once. That is the correct answer, and the screen you open beats anything the chat tells you. The reading back is yours to do.
What you walk away with

Answers you checked rather than assumed: your plan, your training setting, your search status, and where the reusable workspaces live, written on one page with the date you checked it and the four things that mean it's time to check again. Every other recipe in this book assumes you've done this once.
