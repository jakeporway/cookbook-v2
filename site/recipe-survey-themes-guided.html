Three Hundred Survey Answers, Five Themes

Your post-program survey has one open-ended question and 300 answers nobody has read. Paste the column and get back the recurring themes, how often each appears, and verbatim quotes, along with a rule for checking those quotes before anyone gets quoted to a funder.

Nothing to flag, as long as no answer names a person. The assistant checks that with you first
What it will ask you

What you asked people, word for word. Whether any answers name a staff member, a participant, or a site, which is the one thing that stops it (it will send you to the redaction pass (Recipe 01) first). Roughly how many answers there are. Who the summary is for. You never write anything new, and you never have to work out whether your responses will fit in one message: it asks a plain question and works that out itself.

How to use this
  1. Copy the whole block below and paste it into your AI chat

The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my research assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical. Your job is to run the following FOR me, asking me
only questions a non-technical person can answer, and being
strict about quotes, because a quote nobody actually wrote
reads exactly like a real one.

WHAT WE'RE DOING
Reading every answer to one open-ended question from our
survey, and giving me back the handful of themes that
genuinely recur: what each theme is in plain language, how
many people touched it, three word-for-word quotes each,
anything serious that came up only once or twice, and a list
of the answers that fit nowhere, so nothing disappears
quietly.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "how many characters long is your
  response column?" Instead ask "roughly how many people
  answered, and is a typical answer a sentence or a
  paragraph?" and work out from that whether the answers will
  fit in one message or have to be split into batches. If you
  genuinely can't infer something, give me 2-3 plain choices
  to pick from.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then work out how much
  text this chat will take in one message and adapt to it. If
  my answers won't fit, say so plainly now and tell me how
  we'll split them, rather than letting me discover it
  halfway through.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what I keep the
  survey answers in and where the summary has to end up, and
  give me the result in a form I can paste straight in.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. What I actually asked people, word for word.
2. Whether any answers name a person or a place: a staff
   member, a participant, a particular site. If ANY do, stop:
   tell me those names have to come out before the answers go
   into a chat, and point me at the redaction pass recipe in
   this cookbook. My survey respondents never agreed to have
   their words pasted anywhere.
3. Roughly how many answers there are and how long a typical
   one runs, then have me paste them in.
4. Who the summary is for: my board, a funder, my own program
   team. That sets how plain the language has to be.
5. What I keep the answers in, so we can search them later
   when we check the quotes.

HOW TO DO THE WORK (this part is for you, not me)
Read every answer before naming a single theme. Then give me
the 4-6 themes that genuinely recur, each named in plain
language with no research vocabulary; a count of how many
answers touch each theme, roughly ranked by frequency, noting
that one answer can count toward more than one theme; and
three VERBATIM quotes per theme, copied word for word, typos
and all, never paraphrased, trimmed, or grammar-corrected. Do
not invent a theme to reach a rounder number. If something
comes up only two or three times but sounds serious, call it
out separately rather than promoting it to a theme. Finish
with a short "didn't fit any theme" list so nothing
disappears silently.

AFTER THE THEMES, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Reading the serious-but-rare callouts and the "didn't fit"
   list first, because that is where the thing nobody
   expected usually turns up.
2. Spot-checking the quotes. Have me pick a distinctive
   phrase out of each quote and search for it where I keep
   the answers, and tell me how to run that search in the
   software I named. Every quote found word for word means I
   can use all of them. One quote I can't find means we throw
   the quote list out and you rebuild it, because that one
   was two answers blended together.
3. Getting the summary where it has to go, and saving this
   conversation so the next survey cycle takes ten minutes
   rather than an afternoon.

RULES
- Never clean up a quote: typos, grammar, run-on sentences
  and all.
- Never merge two answers into one quote, and never hang a
  quote on a theme it doesn't actually support.
- If I ask you to skip the name check, say no once and tell
  me in one sentence why it matters.
- Before we finish, remind me out loud that a blended or
  misremembered quote in a funder report costs trust that is
  hard to rebuild, so the quotes get checked before anybody
  gets quoted.

IF THE ANSWERS ARE TOO LONG FOR ONE MESSAGE
Don't let me cut them short. Split them into batches of about
150 answers, read each batch, then merge the theme lists
yourself and tell me plainly that the counts are combined
across batches.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
do together, then ask your first question.
Two rules before this leaves your desk. First, if answers name people, they go through the redaction pass (Recipe 01) before anything is pasted. The prompt makes the assistant ask about names before it will read a single answer, but you are the one who knows what is in that column. Second, the assistant is instructed to walk you through checking every verbatim quote against your own file, and that check is still yours to perform. A blended quote in a funder report costs trust that's hard to rebuild.
What you walk away with

A plain-language theme summary with counts, verified verbatim quotes ready for the board deck or funder report, and an "other" pile showing that nothing was quietly dropped. All of the feedback finally gets read.
