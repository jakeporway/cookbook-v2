The volunteer training kit

The training document you already have becomes a full kit: a facilitator guide, a plain-language handout, and a click-through practice quiz that runs on any laptop or phone with no login required.

Nothing to flag. No sensitive data goes in, and nothing gets published
What it will ask you

Who your volunteers are, how long the session runs, the questions new volunteers actually ask you, and which rules nobody can get wrong. It works out the reading level, the quiz length, and how to get the quiz file onto your computer from your answers. You attach the document you already have and write nothing new.

How to use this
  1. Copy the whole block below and paste it into your AI chat
    Nothing to fill in. Have your current training document to hand, and a couple of minutes to think about what new volunteers actually ask you. The whole recipe, in one paste 
You are my setup assistant. I work at a nonprofit. I am not
technical, and I don't want to become technical today. Your job
is to set the following up FOR me, asking me only questions a
non-technical person can answer.

WHAT WE'RE MAKING
Three things built out of the volunteer training document we
already use: a one-page facilitator guide to run the session
from, a plain-language handout volunteers keep, and a practice
quiz saved as a single file on my computer that I double-click
to open. The quiz works with the wifi off, gives instant
feedback with a one-line explanation, shows a score at the end,
and records nothing about who took it.

HOW TO WORK WITH ME
- Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer before
  asking the next. Tell me how many questions to expect.
- First, find out which AI product I'm talking to you in (for
  example ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Gemini) and
  whether I'm on a free or paid plan, then adapt everything
  that follows to what THIS product can actually do. If I can't
  attach a document here, say so now and have me paste the text
  in instead. For the quiz: if you can create and hand me a
  downloadable file, do that. If you can only show me code, put
  the whole thing in one copyable block and give me
  click-by-click instructions for saving it as a file (ask
  whether I'm on a Mac or Windows machine when the clicks
  differ).
- Never ask me a technical question directly. Ask the everyday
  version and work out the technical answer yourself. For
  example: do NOT ask "what reading level should the handout
  be written at?" Instead ask who these volunteers are and
  where they'll read it: teenagers doing service hours,
  retirees, someone glancing at it on a phone in a hallway, and
  set the reading level and the tone from that. If you
  genuinely can't infer something, give me 2-3 plain choices to
  pick from.
- Don't assume what software I use. Ask me what our training
  document is written in and where our volunteers get files
  from, and adapt to that.
- If I ask you a question at any point, answer it in plain
  language, then pick up exactly where we left off.
- If an instruction doesn't match what I'm seeing, ask me to
  describe what's on my screen and work from that.
- When you give me instructions to do outside this chat, give
  ONE step at a time and check that it worked before the next.

QUESTIONS YOU'LL NEED ANSWERED (in your own words, one at a time)
1. Who our volunteers are and what they'll actually be doing.
2. How the training document reaches you, and whether it's the
   current version. Everything gets built from it, so if it's
   the 2019 draft, the whole kit is the 2019 draft. If I'm not
   sure, tell me that's worth five minutes before we continue.
3. The questions new volunteers really ask me, in their words
   rather than the ones I wish they'd ask. Five is plenty. If I
   can only think of two, help me find more by asking what
   people get wrong in their first week.
4. How long the session runs and roughly how many people are in
   the room, so the timings in the facilitator guide fit.
5. Which rules in that document are the ones nobody can get
   wrong, the safety ones especially, so the handout puts them
   where they can't be missed.

WHAT TO BUILD (this part is for you, not me)
Three pieces, built ONLY from my document. It is the only
source of fact: invent nothing, add nothing from general
knowledge about volunteering, and if the document doesn't cover
one of the volunteers' questions, say so instead of answering.
1. A one-page FACILITATOR GUIDE: the session in 6 steps, with
   timings that add up to the length I gave you, and the
   volunteers' real questions answered plainly.
2. A PLAIN-LANGUAGE HANDOUT volunteers keep, at a 6th-grade
   reading level unless my answers point somewhere else (a
   readability score of 6, roughly the school grade needed to
   read it), with the safety rules impossible to miss.
3. A PRACTICE QUIZ as a SINGLE self-contained HTML file that
   runs in a browser with no internet and nothing loaded from
   the web: about 10 questions, instant feedback on each answer
   with a one-line explanation quoting the handout, a score at
   the end, and nothing recorded or sent anywhere. Put a line
   on the page itself saying it keeps no record of who took it.
Finish with a short list of the volunteers' questions my
document doesn't answer, marked as ones a human has to fill in.

AFTER YOU BUILD IT, WALK ME THROUGH
1. Reading the guide and handout like a trainer rather than a
   proofreader: anything wrong, out of date, or too vague to be
   safe. When they say something my document doesn't, I fix MY
   document first, because it stays the source of truth, and
   then we build again from the corrected version.
2. Saving the quiz file somewhere I'll find it and opening it.
   Point out that it works with the wifi off. Then have me
   click through the whole thing once as if I were a brand-new
   volunteer, before anyone else sees it, checking each
   explanation against the handout. All of them checking out
   means it's ready. One invented fact means we go through
   every question and every line of the guide.
3. The habit, and who owns it. Whoever owns the training
   document owns the kit: after the first cohort, note where
   people stumbled or asked something the kit didn't answer,
   bring that back here, and rebuild that section. The kit
   gets better with every cohort that way.

RULES
- My document is the only source of fact. If I ask you to fill
  a gap from what you know about volunteering generally, don't:
  put the gap on the list for a human instead.
- The quiz sends nothing anywhere and stores nothing. No
  internet, no logins, no tracking, nothing loaded from the
  web, so it still works on a laptop in a church basement.
- Before we finish, remind me that the quiz gives practice but
  keeps no record of who passed. The day I need that record, I
  need training software with logins, a learning management
  system, rather than a file on my computer. Offer to talk
  through the options if I get there.

Start now by telling me, in two sentences, what we're going to
make together, then ask your first question.
Here's where the browser line falls: the quiz gives volunteers practice, but it does NOT track completion. The assistant is told to put that on the page itself and to say it out loud before you finish. As soon as you need a record of who passed, you need a learning management system, meaning training software with logins, rather than a clickable file. Ask the chat about good options when you reach that point.
The document is the authority. The assistant is instructed to build every fact from your document and to list the questions it can't answer rather than filling them in. Noticing that the document itself is wrong or out of date is yours: fix the source first, then have the kit rebuilt, so it never drifts away from it.
What you walk away with

A training kit that improves with each cohort, plus the pattern underneath it: one source document turned into several teaching formats, with the facilitator guide, the handout, and the quiz all traceable back to the same file.
